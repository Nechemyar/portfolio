Why every piece is where it is

Satoshi throughout. BrandelLuchador stays in the menu only. Every size is a multiple of the nav label, so the hero and the menu bar always scale together. Turn on "Why" in the bar below to see the numbers on the hero.

  1. 1

    Reel fills the screen

    The video is the whole background, edge to edge, with nothing else competing. The nav and the two corner items float on top of it as their own layer, which is the idea Apple's iOS 26 design is built on: controls sit in a layer above the content.

    When the footage exists, every shot keeps the middle calm and a soft radial shade sits behind the headline. NN/g says to design for the worst frame and to hit 4.5:1 contrast for normal text and 3:1 for large text. The bottom corners get a light floor fade for the same reason.

    Apple newsroom, new design · NN/g, text over images

  2. 2

    Headline alone in the middle

    Satoshi Regular (400), 5.06m on desktop (about 80px at 1440 wide) and 3.4m on phones (about 44px), tracking −0.045em, line height just under 1. This matches what the premium sites in the reference set below actually do: hero lines between 40 and 80px, weight 300 to 400, tight negative tracking, and about 4.5 to 5× the nav label. Oura sets theirs at 80px, weight 300, −0.05em. Not one of them uses bold for the hero line. Light, large type reads as more expensive than heavy type. Try 300 to 700 in the bar.

    Material 3's largest display style is about 4× its label size, so 5× the menu label sits right in that range. It's the only thing in the centre, so the eye has one place to land.

    Reference captures below · Material 3 type scale · Apple HIG, typography

  3. 3

    Bottom left: what you do

    The headline is a hook, so this line says the service in plain words. NN/g's first homepage guideline is a tagline that says what the company does. Their research also shows that defining yourself by a technology ("powered by AI") doesn't tell people what they get, and "What AI can't build" has the same problem without this line beside it.

    Left side because attention leans left: NN/g measured about 80% of viewing time on the left half of the page. Set at the menu size (1m), regular weight like the corner text on Lucid and Polestar, held to 36 characters so it breaks into two short lines.

    NN/g, homepage guidelines · NN/g, "Powered by AI" · NN/g, attention leans left

  4. 4

    Bottom right: see the work, not book a call

    No booking button in the hero. Someone paying £2k+ for a website wants to see your work before they talk to you, and pushing a booking button at them first jumps that step. NN/g watched this happen with "Get Started" buttons: people clicked them before they understood the offer, or stopped looking for information because the button made them think there wasn't any. The MarketingExperiments test that moved a CTA below the fold and added more explanation got 74% more conversions. Their speaker Michael Aagaard's point was that a CTA belongs at the right moment in the visitor's thinking. No fixed rule decides it.

    Booking is still one tap away: CONTACT in the nav stays fixed on every screen, top right, already above the fold. The big Book a call moment comes straight after the work section, when people have just seen proof.

    "See the work ↓" also helps with the full-screen problem in note 5: it tells people the page carries on. Same size and weight as the nav labels. Use the bar to compare it with the old button.

    NN/g, "Get Started" stops users · MarketingExperiments, CTA location · NN/g, scrolling and attention

  5. 5

    Mirrored top and bottom bars

    The bottom row is the same height and side gutter as the nav, so the screen has a frame: two bars at the edges and one headline in the middle. That's the visual balance you asked for. Light on top (MENU, logo, CONTACT), light on the bottom (one line, one button), and nothing between them but the video and the headline.

    Full screen does risk a "false floor" where people think the page ends there, which NN/g warns about. Having real content pinned to the bottom edge helps, and the work section should start straight after with no gap. They found 57% of viewing time goes to the first screen and 74% to the first two, so the second screen still matters.

    NN/g, the fold manifesto · NN/g, scrolling and attention

  6. +

    The video, when it lands

    Muted, inline, looping. A poster frame shows until it plays, and it only starts loading while the loader runs. web.dev notes that the first frame can count as the page's main paint, so a light poster keeps the speed score healthy. WebM first with MP4 as the fallback, audio stripped. One continuous reel, not a slideshow: Baymard found most people never see past the first slide of a rotating hero.

    web.dev, video performance · Baymard, homepage carousels

  7. +

    Pause control

    Once the reel is in, a small pause button goes in next to "See the work", the way Porsche does it. WCAG 2.2.2 requires a way to pause anything that moves for more than five seconds.

    WCAG 2.2.2, pause, stop, hide
